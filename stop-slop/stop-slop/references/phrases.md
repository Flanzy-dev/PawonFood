# Phrases to Remove

## Throat-Clearing Openers

Remove announcement phrases that delay the point, including "Here's the thing," "It turns out," "Let me be clear," and "The truth is." State the content directly.

## Emphasis Crutches

Delete phrases that add emphasis without meaning, including "Full stop," "Let that sink in," "Make no mistake," and "Here's why that matters."

## Business Jargon

| Avoid | Prefer |
|-------|--------|
| Navigate challenges | Handle or address challenges |
| Unpack an analysis | Explain or examine |
| Lean into | Accept or embrace |
| Landscape | Situation or field |
| Game-changer | Name the specific effect |
| Double down | Commit or increase |
| Deep dive | Analysis or examination |
| Moving forward | Next or from now on |
| Circle back | Return to or revisit |
| On the same page | Aligned or agreed |

## Empty Adverbs and Hedges

Remove adverbs when they only intensify or soften a claim. Common offenders include "really," "just," "literally," "genuinely," "honestly," "simply," "actually," "deeply," "truly," "fundamentally," "inherently," "inevitably," "interestingly," "importantly," and "crucially."

Keep an adverb when it changes the meaning, specifies frequency or manner, or preserves an evidence-based qualification.

Also examine filler phrases such as "At its core," "In today's world," "It's worth noting," "At the end of the day," "When it comes to," "In a world where," and "The reality is."

## Meta-Commentary

Remove self-referential previews when the text can move directly to the content. Examples include "The rest of this essay explains," "Let me walk you through," "In this section, we'll," and "As we'll see."

Keep structural signposting when readers need it to follow a long academic, legal, or technical document.

## Vague Declarations

Replace claims such as "The implications are significant" or "The stakes are high" with the specific implication, affected group, mechanism, or measurable consequence.
